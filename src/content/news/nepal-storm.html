A major snowfall event over the Nepal Himalaya began on 24 September and runs through Sunday. On SnowMapper's own three-day snowfall metric it is the largest event in the twelve months the system has been running — larger than the October 2025 storm that prompted Nepal's first national action plan on snowfall and cold waves. Almost all of it falls above 4,000 m.

What the forecast shows

The 04:54 UTC cycle on 24 September places a four-day snowfall event over the length of the Nepal Himalaya, peaking on Saturday 26 September. Six of Nepal's seven provinces reach the highest warning level; only Madhesh, which has no high terrain, is untouched.

From forecast to warning

The same forecast is graded into five hazard levels for every district, province and river basin. On 26 September, 16 districts reach Very high and 5 reach High, with 8 more carrying a lower-confidence upgrade where the ensemble grades them above the deterministic run.

It is a high-mountain event

This is the qualification that matters most. Across every affected district the snow sits in the top two elevation bands. On the peak day the 5,000 m and 6,000 m bands take 60–80 cm, the 4,000 m band takes at most 17 cm, and the 3,000 m band and everything below it take nothing at all. The lowest the snow line reaches anywhere in the country is 3,629 m.

So the exposure is passes, high routes, herders and glaciated terrain — not the settled mid-hills. What the mid-hills and Terai get instead is rain, peaking at 172 mm in 24 hours on 25 September.

Falling on Rasuwa

The district forecast to take the most snow is Rasuwa — 171 cm in the 72 hours to 26 September, joint highest in the country. It is also the district that, on 26 August, less than a month ago, was struck by the ice-and-rock avalanche on the China–Nepal border that fluidised into a flood and killed more than 1,400 people, with almost 6,000 still missing, in what looks set to be the largest Himalayan disaster on record. Mountain Futures' Simon Allen was among the scientists who assessed that event. Relief and recovery operations in the district are still under way.

Read that table carefully, because the bottom row is the one that mattered. The remnants of Cyclone Montha rank last on peak accumulation, yet they were by far the most damaging storm in the record — the one that killed people and drove the national action plan. Two things the peak total does not capture explain why: the snow fell lower, a median snow line of 4,011 m against 4,334 m in the larger early-October event three weeks before, and it fell at the end of the trekking season onto routes that were occupied.

That is the honest limitation of the grading. It measures how unusual the snowfall is for the place and time of year. It does not measure who is standing underneath it. On the amount metric the current forecast is 2.3× the Montha peak and puts 16 districts at a level that storm never reached anywhere — but the thing that made October 2025 lethal was elevation and exposure, and those are judgements the system hands to a forecaster rather than making itself.

An unplanned test

SnowMapper is being built for Nepal under Work Package 2 of the BARHKH project, with a deployment mission to Kathmandu planned for November. This event arrived two months early. The forecast chain ran end to end on a real storm, the district warnings were read and passed on by partners in Kathmandu the same day through the technical working group set up a week earlier.

It also shows what is still missing. The warning thresholds are provisional and have not been agreed with a warning authority, and no observations are available in real time to verify any of this. Both are on the agenda for November.

How to read this

  • This is a forecast, not an observation. Everything here comes from the model cycle of 24 September 2026, 04:54 UTC.
  • The thresholds are provisional. They are calibrated to this model over 24 water years and say how unusual a forecast is, not what impact to expect. They have not been agreed with a warning authority, and this is not an official warning.
  • Snowfall is modelled water equivalent converted to depth at 100 kg/m³, not a measured snow depth.
  • The model runs wet over Nepal in the monsoon. Late September is monsoon withdrawal, so these totals should be treated as an upper bound until observations say otherwise.
